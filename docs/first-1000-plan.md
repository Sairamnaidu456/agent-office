# First $1,000 operating plan

This is a testable business hypothesis, not a revenue forecast. The Office can do research and production work; customers decide whether it earns anything. Count a milestone only from money actually settled to the founder, after processor fees and refunds. Do not count projected sales, pending checkouts, or account balances that cannot be withdrawn.

## Start with a service, then productize what repeats

**Buyer:** solo developers and small software teams already using coding agents, who lose time getting a reliable multi-agent workflow running.

**Pilot offer:** a fixed-scope “Coding Agent Office Setup” session. In one 90-minute session, configure the customer's own Agent Office/Codex workspace, establish isolated task work, set a practical approval profile, create a small task queue, and leave a tested handoff/runbook. The customer signs into their own provider accounts and enters all secrets themselves. Never ask a customer to send passwords, API keys, banking details, or identity documents.

**Pilot price hypothesis:** $199, paid through a hosted checkout before scheduling. Seven sales are $1,393 gross; fees, refunds and tax treatment affect actual proceeds. Reconcile the provider payout before declaring the $1,000 milestone. The offer and price are hypotheses until real buyers respond.

This service is the initial experiment because it requires no inventory, paid ads, domain, or product infrastructure. It can produce customer feedback while the setup checklist is improved. It still requires founder time to deliver and customer trust; agents cannot replace those.

## Validate before building more

1. Make a short demo using a clean, synthetic repository and fake credentials. Show the task queue, isolated worktree, approval boundary and successful test/build; do not record customer data.
2. Prepare a one-page offer, scope, exclusions, schedule, cancellation/refund terms, privacy note and fulfillment checklist. Leave checkout disabled until a founder-owned merchant account is verified.
3. Identify ten relevant developers or teams through public, relevant communities and personal professional contacts. Prepare individually tailored messages; do not scrape personal data, post automatically, or send bulk messages.
4. Ask about their current setup pain before pitching. If at least three describe the same costly problem, offer the paid pilot. If twenty qualified conversations produce no paid booking, revise the buyer or offer before building a standalone product.
5. Deliver the first booking manually, record time spent and friction, then update the checklist. Seek permission before using a customer's name, quote, repository, or result as a public case study.

## Keep the initial spend at $0

- Use the already available local Office and existing ChatGPT plan for the first experiment. ChatGPT plan usage has limits; it is not an unlimited unattended inference budget.
- Use a private demo and existing channels. No domain, cloud VM, ad campaign, inventory, wallet, crypto account, or paid software is needed to test interest.
- Add a hosted checkout only after the founder creates and verifies the merchant account and payout method. Use the provider's hosted checkout; never collect card details in Agent Office.
- Do not claim the repository's budget guard covers Codex usage or subscription allowances. If spend data is absent or untracked, report **unknown**, not zero. Stop any work that could add a charge when its cost cannot be determined.

## Make the $100,000 target a second-stage objective

Treat $100,000 as cumulative settled net proceeds, not ARR, a valuation, or gross checkout volume. First prove that the pilot solves a repeatable problem: complete three paid deliveries, measure delivery time and support burden, and confirm that the same checklist works for multiple customers. Then test one scalable extension—an agency/team package, paid setup kit, or maintenance plan—only when customers ask for the repeated value. Do not hire contractors, buy ads, or build a subscription product until paid demand supports those costs.

## CEO authority and operating boundaries

The CEO can select experiments, set priorities, prepare assets, assign workers, run local checks and report results without waiting for founder approval. Keep hard boundaries around money, identity, legal commitments, publication, customer data and irreversible account actions. Workers must use their own worktrees; QA checks changes before a release. A worker's claim that a task is complete is not evidence of a sale, payout, passing build or deployment.

Run the first cycle locally:

1. Check the budget and mark telemetry unavailable where the provider is not tracked.
2. Research the buyer and competing offers; cite sources and label uncertain assumptions.
3. Prepare the demo and sales materials.
4. Validate with real conversations and record objections without storing unnecessary personal data.
5. Deliver a paid pilot only after checkout and scope are verified.
6. Reconcile charges, fees, refunds and bank payout; update the revenue ledger from provider evidence.
7. Review whether the offer earned positive net proceeds for the time spent before starting another cycle.

Do not describe the Office as a 24/7 money-making system. The local worker queue runs only while the Office process and its authenticated provider sessions are available; model limits, computer uptime and provider permissions still apply.
